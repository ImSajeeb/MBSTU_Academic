# 2. HOW TO ADAPT A MASTER ANSWER IN THE EXAM
- Start with the definition or main concept.
- Write the major points from the relevant Master Answer.
- Use the scenario given in the question as the example instead of changing the whole answer.
- For comparison questions, clearly state the difference between the theories or concepts.
- For ethical decision questions, identify stakeholders, possible harms, duties, alternatives, and the final
responsible action.
- For flowchart questions, reproduce the flow and briefly explain every stage.
- End with a clear conclusion or recommendation.
Memory pattern: Definition → Main principles → Scenario application → Ethical reasoning →
Conclusion

# MASTER ANSWER–1
Ethics, Law and Professional Responsibility
Applicable to: Q1(a), Q1(c), Q1(d), Q20(a), Q25(a), Q25(b)

Applicable to:
Q1(a), Q1(c), Q1(d), Q20(a), Q25(a), Q25(b)

## English Answer
Ethics, law, and professional responsibility are related, but they are not the same thing.

Ethics means the principles that help us decide what is right and wrong. Ethical behavior may require a
person to do something even when there is no specific law requiring it. For example, a software company
may legally collect some user data, but using that data without respecting users' reasonable
expectations may still be unethical.

Law means the rules created and enforced by the government. A computing professional must follow
laws related to privacy, cybersecurity, intellectual property, contracts, and data protection. Breaking the
law can result in legal punishment.

Professional responsibility means the duties that a person has because of their professional role. A
software engineer is expected to protect users, maintain competence, be honest, protect privacy and
security, and avoid causing unnecessary harm.

These three areas can sometimes lead to different decisions. For example, suppose a mobile health
application collects heart-rate, location, and lifestyle information. The marketing department wants to
use this data for targeted advertising.

The law may allow some form of data processing if the required legal conditions are satisfied. However,
from an ethical point of view, using sensitive health-related information for advertising without clear
consent may be wrong. From a professional responsibility point of view, the software engineers should
consider users' privacy and should not simply support a decision that can seriously harm users.

Therefore, being legal does not always mean being ethical. A professional should consider law, ethics,
and professional duties together before making an important decision.


# MASTER ANSWER–2
Historical Cases of Ethical Failure in Technology
Applicable to: Q1(b)

Applicable to:
Q1(b)

## English Answer
There have been many cases where technology was used or developed without properly considering
ethical responsibilities. Two important examples are the Therac-25 accidents and the Volkswagen
emissions scandal.

# 1. Therac-25

Therac-25 was a computerized radiation therapy machine used to treat cancer patients. Due to software
and system design problems, the machine sometimes delivered extremely high radiation doses to
patients.

The major ethical failure was the failure to properly consider safety, testing, software reliability, and
communication of known problems. The system was used in a high-risk medical environment, so


developers and organizations had a strong responsibility to protect patients.

Technology involved: Computer-controlled radiation therapy machine.

Ethical failure: Inadequate safety design, testing, risk management, and response to reported problems.

Stakeholders affected: Patients, doctors, hospitals, software and hardware developers, and the
manufacturer.

Lesson learned: Software used in safety-critical systems must be tested carefully, known problems must
be taken seriously, and public safety must be given higher priority than convenience or cost.

# 2. Volkswagen Emissions Scandal

Volkswagen used software in some diesel vehicles to detect emissions testing conditions and change the
behavior of the vehicle during tests. This made the vehicles appear to meet emission standards while
actual emissions could be much higher during normal driving.

The ethical failure involved dishonesty, misuse of software, and putting organizational goals above public
and environmental interests.

Technology involved: Vehicle control software and emissions-control systems.

Ethical failure: Deliberate manipulation of software to mislead regulators and consumers.

Stakeholders     affected:   Customers,      regulators,    employees,      competitors,   communities,   and   the
environment.

Lesson learned: Software professionals must be honest and must not use technical knowledge to deceive
users, regulators, or the public.


# MASTER ANSWER–3
Software Engineering Ethics and Professional Responsibility
Applicable to: Q1(c), Q1(d), Q4(a), Q4(b), Q4(c), Q4(d), Q25(d)

Applicable to:
Q1(c), Q1(d), Q4(a), Q4(b), Q4(c), Q4(d), Q25(d)

## English Answer
Software engineering ethics means applying moral and professional principles while designing,
developing, testing, deploying, and maintaining software.

A software engineer should understand that software can affect people's safety, privacy, money,
employment, education, and daily life. Therefore, technical success alone is not enough.

The major principles include:

# 1. Public Safety and Public Interest

Software professionals should give importance to the safety and well-being of the public. If a system has
a serious safety or security problem, releasing it only to meet a deadline may be irresponsible.

# 2. Privacy

Developers should respect users' personal information. Data should not be collected or used
unnecessarily, and sensitive information should be properly protected.

# 3. Security

Professionals should identify security weaknesses and take reasonable steps to fix them. Known serious
vulnerabilities should not be ignored simply because fixing them is expensive or time-consuming.

# 4. Honesty


Software professionals should be truthful about system performance, limitations, risks, and testing
results. They should not hide important problems from users or decision-makers.

# 5. Competence

Professionals should have sufficient knowledge and skills for their responsibilities. They should also
recognize their limitations and seek help when necessary.

6. Fairness

Software should not unfairly discriminate against individuals or groups. Developers should consider
possible bias in data, algorithms, and system design.

7. Responsibility to Users

Engineers should consider the real effects of their systems on users. They should not treat users only as
sources of profit or data.

8. Accountability

Professionals should accept responsibility for their decisions and should keep proper records of important
risks, tests, and decisions.

Professional responsibility becomes especially important when organizational pressure conflicts with
public interest. For example, if testing shows serious security weaknesses, an engineer should report the
problem and recommend fixing it before release.


# MASTER ANSWER–4
Utilitarianism and Deontology
Applicable to: Q2(a), Q20(b), Q24(b), Q25(c)

Applicable to:
Q2(a), Q20(b), Q24(b), Q25(c)

## English Answer
Two important ethical frameworks are Utilitarianism and Deontology.

## Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism judges an action mainly by its consequences. The basic idea is that we should choose the
action that produces the greatest overall benefit and the least overall harm.

For example, suppose a social-media company wants to analyze private messages and contact networks
to increase user engagement. A utilitarian may compare the benefits, such as better recommendations
and improved services, with the harms, such as privacy loss, misuse of personal information, and
possible security risks.

If the privacy harms are much greater than the benefits, the practice should not be accepted.

## Deontology

Deontology focuses on duties, rules, and rights rather than only consequences. Kantian ethics especially
emphasizes treating people as ends in themselves, not merely as tools for achieving another goal.

In the social-media example, users have rights and expectations regarding their private information.
Using private messages for another purpose without meaningful consent may violate their autonomy and
privacy, even if the company gains financial benefits.

Main Difference

## Social Contract Theory


Social Contract Theory considers the moral rules and expectations that people accept so that society can
function fairly and safely. In computing, this can be used to ask whether a technology respects
reasonable expectations between users, organizations, and society.

For example, users may reasonably expect a social-media platform to protect private information and not
use it in unexpected ways. An action that violates these reasonable expectations can be ethically
problematic even when it provides business benefits.

Therefore, when analyzing an ethical dilemma, Social Contract Theory can be considered together with
Utilitarianism, Deontology, and Virtue Ethics.


# MASTER ANSWER–5
## Virtue Ethics
Applicable to: Q2(b), Q20(b), Q24(b), Q25(c)

Applicable to:
Q2(b), Q20(b), Q24(b), Q25(c)

## English Answer
Virtue Ethics focuses on the character of the person making the decision rather than only the rules or
consequences.

A good software professional should develop qualities such as:

Honesty: Tell the truth about system limitations, risks, and problems.

Fairness: Treat users and different groups equally and avoid discrimination.

Responsibility: Accept responsibility for professional decisions and their effects.

Courage: Speak up when a serious ethical or technical problem is discovered, even when doing so is
difficult.

Respect: Respect users' privacy, rights, dignity, and choices.

For example, if an engineer discovers a serious vulnerability before product release, a virtuous engineer
will not hide it just to satisfy a manager. The engineer will honestly report the problem and try to protect
users.

Thus, Virtue Ethics asks an important question: "What would a good and responsible professional do in
this situation?"


# MASTER ANSWER–6
Ethical Decision-Making Process
Applicable to: Q2(c), Q12(d), Q15(d), Q20(d), Q21(e), Q24(f), Q25(j)

Applicable to:
Q2(c), Q12(d), Q15(d), Q20(d), Q21(e), Q24(f), Q25(j)

## English Answer
An ethical decision should not be made suddenly. A systematic process helps a professional understand
the problem and choose a responsible action.

Step 1: Identify the Ethical Issue

First, clearly identify what is wrong or what ethical conflict exists.

For example, a company may want to collect personal data without proper consent.


Step 2: Identify Stakeholders and Potential Harms

Stakeholders may include users, employees, customers, developers, management, government, and the
wider society.

The professional should identify possible harms such as privacy loss, financial loss, discrimination, safety
problems, or security risks.

Step 3: Apply Ethical Principles or Frameworks

The situation can be examined using Utilitarianism, Deontology, Virtue Ethics, professional codes, and
principles such as privacy, fairness, honesty, and public interest.

Step 4: Check Legal and Professional Obligations

The professional should check relevant laws, contracts, organizational policies, professional codes,
standards, and license requirements.

Step 5: Evaluate Alternatives

Different possible actions should be considered. For example:

Launch immediately

Delay the launch

Fix the problem first

Reduce the system's scope

Conduct additional testing

Ask an independent expert for review

Step 6: Select and Justify the Most Ethical Action

The final decision should be based on evidence, ethical principles, risks, and responsibilities.

Step 7: Implement, Monitor and Review

After making the decision, the system should be monitored. If new problems appear, they should be
corrected.


# MASTER ANSWER–7
ACM Code of Ethics
Applicable to: Q4(a), Q4(b), Q4(c), Q4(d), Q6(a), Q15(d), Q25(d)

Applicable to:
Q4(a), Q4(b), Q4(c), Q4(d), Q6(a), Q15(d), Q25(d)

## English Answer
The ACM Code of Ethics provides ethical principles for computing professionals. It helps professionals
make responsible decisions when technical, organizational, and social interests conflict.

Some important ACM principles are:

# 1. Contribute to Society and Human Well-Being

Computing professionals should use their knowledge to benefit society and reduce harm.

# 2. Avoid Harm

Professionals should try to prevent or reduce negative consequences caused by computing systems.

# 3. Be Honest and Trustworthy

Professionals should communicate honestly about systems, limitations, risks, and professional decisions.


# 4. Be Fair and Take Action Not to Discriminate

Computing systems should not unfairly discriminate against people or groups.

# 5. Respect Privacy

Professionals should respect individuals' privacy and carefully handle personal information.

6. Honor Confidentiality

Confidential information should not be disclosed or misused without proper authority.

7. Maintain Professional Competence

Professionals should develop and maintain the knowledge and skills required for their work.

8. Follow Professional Responsibilities

Professionals should follow relevant laws, rules, standards, and professional responsibilities.

Professional codes are important because they provide guidance when a situation is not fully covered by
law. However, a code does not replace professional judgment. Engineers still need to understand the
situation, evaluate risks, and make a responsible decision.

Example: Security Problem Before Release

If testing shows serious security weaknesses but management wants immediate release, the engineer
should consider:

Avoiding harm

Public interest

Privacy and security

Honesty

Professional responsibility

Therefore, the engineer should clearly report the risk and recommend remediation before release if the
vulnerability can seriously harm users.

## Professional Workplace Culture

Professional ethics also includes the way people are treated inside technology organizations. A healthy
workplace should support respectful behavior, fair treatment, diversity, inclusion, and the ability to raise
ethical concerns.

A professional who raises a genuine technical or ethical concern should be taken seriously rather than
being discouraged simply because the concern may delay a project.


# MASTER ANSWER–8
Professional Dissent and Whistleblowing
Applicable to: Q5(a), Q5(b), Q5(c), Q5(d), Q20(e), Q22(d), Q25(i)

Applicable to:
Q5(a), Q5(b), Q5(c), Q5(d), Q20(e), Q22(d), Q25(i)

## English Answer
Professional dissent means expressing disagreement with a professional decision when a person believes
that the decision may be unsafe, unethical, illegal, or harmful.

Whistleblowing means reporting serious wrongdoing to an appropriate authority, sometimes outside the
organization when internal reporting has failed.


For example, if a cybersecurity analyst discovers that a company knowingly sells software with a serious
vulnerability, the analyst should first verify the facts and report the issue through appropriate internal
channels.

Before external disclosure, the analyst should consider:

Is the problem real and supported by evidence?

How serious is the possible harm?

Has the organization been informed?

Has the organization had a reasonable opportunity to fix the problem?

Is external disclosure necessary to protect users?

Can disclosure itself create additional harm?

What legal and professional obligations apply?

Whistleblowing can create risks for the person involved. These may include job loss, damage to
professional reputation, organizational retaliation, legal disputes, financial problems, and personal stress.

However, if serious wrongdoing continues and users remain at significant risk, external reporting may
become ethically justified.

The disclosure should be limited to what is necessary to protect affected people. Sensitive information
should not be unnecessarily exposed.


# MASTER ANSWER–9
Privacy, Personal Data and Informed Consent
Applicable to: Q1(a), Q2(a), Q6(a), Q6(b), Q6(c), Q6(d), Q14(a), Q14(b), Q14(c), Q14(d), Q15(a), Q20(a), Q21(a),
Q21(c), Q24(c), Q25(e)

Applicable to:
Q1(a), Q2(a), Q6(a), Q6(b), Q6(c), Q6(d), Q14(a), Q14(b), Q14(c), Q14(d), Q15(a), Q20(a), Q21(a), Q21(c),
Q24(c), Q25(e)

## English Answer
Privacy means respecting a person's control over personal information and protecting that information
from unnecessary collection, use, or disclosure.

Personal data may include names, location, health information, browsing behavior, contact networks,
biometric information, and other information that can identify or describe a person.

Informed Consent

Informed consent means that a person understands what data will be collected, why it will be collected,
how it will be used, who may receive it, and what important consequences may occur before agreeing to
the collection.

Simply clicking "Accept" does not always mean meaningful informed consent.

For example, if an application has a very long privacy policy but does not clearly tell users that precise
location will be continuously collected and shared with third parties, the consent may not be meaningful.

Good consent should be:

# 1. Clear

Information should be written in understandable language.

# 2. Transparent

Users should know what data is collected and how it is used.


# 3. Voluntary

Users should not be unfairly forced to agree to unnecessary data collection.

# 4. Specific

Consent should clearly identify important purposes of data processing.

# 5. Understandable

Users should have a reasonable opportunity to understand the consequences.

6. Revocable

Where appropriate, users should have a practical way to withdraw consent.

## Data Minimization

Only necessary data should be collected.

## Purpose Limitation

Data collected for one purpose should not automatically be reused for unrelated purposes.

## Security

Personal information should be protected against unauthorized access, modification, and disclosure.

Transparency

Organizations should explain their data practices clearly.

Accountability

Organizations should be able to show that they have followed responsible data practices.

## Better Consent Process

A better mobile application could:

Clearly state that precise location will be collected.

Explain why continuous collection is needed.

Clearly identify third-party sharing.

Ask for consent separately for sensitive or optional purposes.

Provide simple privacy settings.

Allow users to withdraw optional consent.

Avoid collecting unnecessary information.

Protect collected information using appropriate security controls.


# MASTER ANSWER–10
Transparency, Explainability and Black-Box AI
Applicable to: Q3(a), Q3(b), Q3(c), Q3(d), Q21(a), Q21(c), Q21(d), Q24(c), Q25(f)

Applicable to:
Q3(a), Q3(b), Q3(c), Q3(d), Q21(a), Q21(c), Q21(d), Q24(c), Q25(f)

## English Answer
Transparency means providing meaningful information about how an AI system is designed, used, and
governed.

Explainability means being able to give understandable reasons for an AI system's important outputs or
decisions.


These are especially important in high-stakes areas such as:

Loan approval

Healthcare

Employment

Criminal justice

Education

Public services

If an AI system rejects a person's loan application but the organization cannot explain why, the person
may not know whether the decision was based on relevant information or unfair factors.

Why Explainability Is Important

Procedural fairness: People should have a reasonable opportunity to understand how important decisions
affecting them are made.

Accountability: Organizations need to identify who is responsible when an automated system causes
harm.

Trust: People are more likely to trust systems when they receive understandable information about
important decisions.

Right to challenge: A person cannot effectively challenge an incorrect decision if they do not know the
reason for it.

Societal Harms of Black-Box AI

# 1. Discrimination

A model may unfairly treat certain communities differently.

# 2. Wrong Decisions

Incorrect predictions can affect loans, jobs, healthcare, education, or legal decisions.

# 3. Lack of Accountability

It becomes difficult to identify why a harmful decision happened.

# 4. Loss of Public Trust

People may lose confidence in institutions using unexplained automated systems.

Information That Should Be Given

An affected person should ideally receive:

The fact that AI was used

The general purpose of the system

Important factors considered

A meaningful explanation of the decision

Information about possible errors or limitations

How to request human review

How to challenge or appeal the decision

The explanation does not necessarily require revealing every line of source code. It should provide
enough meaningful information for the person to understand and challenge the decision.


# MASTER ANSWER–11


Algorithmic Bias and Fairness
Applicable to: Q11(a), Q11(b), Q11(c), Q11(d), Q21(a), Q21(b), Q21(c), Q24(d), Q25(f)

Applicable to:
Q11(a), Q11(b), Q11(c), Q11(d), Q21(a), Q21(b), Q21(c), Q24(d), Q25(f)

## English Answer
Algorithmic bias occurs when an algorithm systematically produces unfair results for certain individuals
or groups.

Bias does not always come from intentional discrimination. It can arise from the data, model, design, or
way the system is used.

## Causes of Algorithmic Bias

# 1. Biased Training Data

If historical training data contains unfair patterns, the model may learn those patterns.

For example, if a company historically hired fewer people from a particular group, an AI trained on old
hiring decisions may learn to rank that group lower.

# 2. Historical Discrimination

Past human discrimination can become part of the training data.

# 3. Proxy Variables

Even if an explicit demographic variable is removed, another variable may indirectly represent it.

For example, location, school, postcode, or employment history may act as a proxy for demographic
characteristics.

# 4. Sampling Problems

If some groups are poorly represented in the training data, the model may perform worse for them.

# 5. Inappropriate Model Objectives

A system optimized only for engagement, profit, speed, or prediction accuracy may produce unfair
outcomes.

Detecting and Measuring Bias

Bias can be examined by comparing system performance across different groups.

Developers can compare:

Selection rates

Error rates

False positives

False negatives

Accuracy

Precision and recall

Other appropriate fairness measures

The system should be tested before deployment and monitored after deployment.

## Improving Fairness

Technical measures include:

Improving training data

Increasing representation


Removing inappropriate features

Testing proxy variables

Using fairness-aware methods

Conducting subgroup testing

Regular model evaluation

Organizational measures include:

Independent review

Clear fairness policies

Documentation

Human oversight

User complaints and appeal mechanisms

Continuous monitoring

Why Removing Demographic Variables Is Not Enough

Suppose gender is removed from a recruitment model. Other variables such as job history, location,
education, or employment gaps may indirectly correlate with gender.

Therefore, removing one explicit variable does not necessarily remove discrimination.

## Gender and Race Bias

Algorithmic bias can reproduce existing social inequalities related to gender or race. A model trained on
historical decisions may learn patterns that reflect unequal treatment in the past.

## Facial Recognition Example

Facial-recognition systems may show different error rates across demographic groups when training data
or testing data are not sufficiently representative. Therefore, performance should be evaluated across
relevant groups rather than relying only on an overall accuracy value.

The ethical response should include representative data, subgroup testing, fairness evaluation,
documentation, human oversight, and continuous monitoring.


# MASTER ANSWER–12
Accountability in Autonomous Systems
Applicable to: Q12(a), Q12(b), Q12(c), Q12(d), Q21(d), Q25(a), Q25(j)

Applicable to:
Q12(a), Q12(b), Q12(c), Q12(d), Q21(d), Q25(a), Q25(j)

## English Answer
Accountability means that people and organizations involved in developing, deploying, and operating an
autonomous system should be responsible for their decisions and actions.

An autonomous system may make decisions automatically, but this does not mean that humans have no
responsibility.

Different people may have different responsibilities.

Developers

Developers are responsible for appropriate implementation, safe design, and addressing known technical
problems.

Testers


Testers should properly test the system and report important failures.

Managers

Managers are responsible for decisions about resources, deadlines, risk acceptance, and deployment.

Manufacturers

Manufacturers should ensure that products are properly designed, tested, documented, and maintained.

Operators

Operators should use the system according to proper procedures and respond to warnings or failures.

Organizations

Organizations have overall responsibilities for governance, policies, risk management, monitoring, and
remediation.

The statement "the AI made the decision" does not automatically remove human responsibility because
humans designed the system, selected data, created objectives, approved deployment, and controlled its
use.

## Accountability Chain

Requirements → Data Collection → Model Design → Implementation → Testing → Deployment →
Monitoring → Incident Response → Remediation

Failure can occur at any stage.

For example:

Wrong requirement

Poor training data

Incorrect model design

Programming error

Insufficient testing

Unsafe deployment

Poor monitoring

Delayed incident response

Therefore, accountability should exist throughout the entire system lifecycle.


# MASTER ANSWER–13
Responsible Disclosure and Cybersecurity Ethics
Applicable to: Q9(a), Q9(b), Q9(c), Q9(d), Q22(a), Q22(b), Q22(c), Q22(d), Q22(e), Q25(h)

Applicable to:
Q9(a), Q9(b), Q9(c), Q9(d), Q22(a), Q22(b), Q22(c), Q22(d), Q22(e), Q25(h)

## English Answer
Responsible disclosure means reporting a cybersecurity vulnerability to the responsible organization in a
way that gives the organization a reasonable opportunity to investigate and fix the problem while
minimizing harm to users.

It is different from unauthorized exploitation or cybercrime.

A responsible security researcher should follow these steps:

# 1. Confirm the Vulnerability Safely


The researcher should verify that the vulnerability is real without causing unnecessary damage.

# 2. Avoid Unnecessary Access

The researcher should not access real users' private information when it is not necessary to demonstrate
the problem.

# 3. Preserve Evidence

Important technical evidence should be recorded so that the organization can understand and reproduce
the problem.

# 4. Identify the Responsible Organization

The vulnerability should be reported to the appropriate vendor, security team, or responsible authority.

# 5. Report Securely

Sensitive technical details should be communicated through an appropriate secure channel.

6. Allow Reasonable Time for Remediation

The organization should receive a reasonable opportunity to investigate and fix the vulnerability.

7. Coordinate Disclosure

If public disclosure is considered, it should be carefully coordinated so that disclosure does not
unnecessarily expose users to attacks.

8. Protect Affected Users

The main objective should be reducing harm rather than gaining publicity.

A researcher should provide enough technical information to reproduce and fix the problem but should
avoid unnecessary personal data, destructive testing, or exploitation.

Ethical Hacking vs Cybercrime
Applicable to: Q10(a), Q10(b), Q10(c), Q10(d), Q25(h)

Applicable to:
Q10(a), Q10(b), Q10(c), Q10(d), Q25(h)

## English Answer
Ethical hacking means authorized security testing performed to identify and fix security weaknesses.

Cybercrime involves unauthorized or illegal activities such as stealing information, damaging systems,
fraud, unauthorized access, or disrupting services.

The main difference is authorization, purpose, and scope.

Ethical hacking normally requires:

Authorization

The organization must give permission before testing.

Defined Scope

The tester should know exactly which systems, applications, accounts, and activities are allowed.

Defined Time

Testing should be performed during approved periods when required.

Purpose

The purpose should be improving security rather than causing harm or obtaining unauthorized benefits.


Proportionality

Testing should be limited to what is reasonably necessary.

Privacy

Testers should avoid unnecessary access to personal information.

In the university example, if the team is authorized to test selected web applications during specified
hours, they cannot access student records unless that activity is explicitly authorized.

They also cannot intentionally shut down university services if disruption is outside the agreed scope.

Technical capability alone does not make hacking ethical. A person may have the technical ability to
access a system, but without authorization that access can still be unethical or illegal.

## English Answer
Copyright, patents, and trademarks are different forms of intellectual-property protection.

Copyright

Copyright primarily protects original creative expression.

In computing, it can apply to:

Source code

Documentation

User-interface artwork

Graphics

Written materials

Copyright generally protects the expression of an idea rather than the underlying abstract idea itself.

Patent

A patent may protect a qualifying new and inventive technical invention, subject to applicable law and
requirements.

For example, a novel hardware-assisted technical method or image-compression technique may
potentially be suitable for patent protection depending on the jurisdiction and legal requirements.

Trademark

A trademark protects names, logos, symbols, or other identifiers used to distinguish goods or services.

For example, a unique company or product name and logo may be protected as trademarks.

Example

Suppose a company creates:

Original source code → Copyright

New technical invention → Potentially patent

Unique product name/logo → Trademark


A company may need several types of protection because different parts of a product have different legal
characteristics.

Importance of IP Protection

Intellectual-property protection can:

Encourage innovation

Protect creators

Support investment

Prevent unauthorized copying

Provide commercial value

However, excessive IP restrictions can also create ethical concerns. They may restrict education,
research, interoperability, competition, or access to useful technology.

Developers have a professional responsibility not to copy protected source code, documentation,
designs, or inventions without proper permission or legal basis.

Greater commercial control

Potential concerns include:

Less transparency

Limited user control

Attribution

Inclusion of license text

Disclosure of modifications

Source-code availability under certain conditions

Identify the affected code.

Correct documentation and attribution.

Review the project for other licensing problems.

## English Answer
The digital divide refers to the gap between people or communities in their ability to access and

It is not only about having an internet connection.

Some people may not have internet access, computers, smartphones, or other necessary devices.

# 2. Skills Divide

Some people may have access to technology but lack the digital skills needed to use it effectively.

Two people may both have internet access, but one may have fast and reliable broadband while another

employment, healthcare, government services, and economic opportunities.

The digital divide can affect:

Education: Students without reliable internet may miss online classes and learning resources.

Employment: People may have fewer opportunities to apply for jobs or develop skills.

Healthcare: People may have difficulty accessing online health information or services.

Government services: Online-only services may exclude people who cannot use them.

Economic opportunity: Businesses and workers without digital access may lose opportunities.

## Solutions

Governments and organizations can:

Improve rural infrastructure

Reduce internet costs

Provide affordable devices

Improve digital literacy

Design accessible systems

Provide community support centers

Offer offline or alternative service channels

Internet connectivity alone is not enough because people also need devices, skills, affordability,
accessibility, quality access, and support.

Actual Conflict

A real personal interest directly conflicts with professional duties.

Potential Conflict

A situation may create a conflict in the future.

Perceived Conflict

Disclose the relationship.

Avoid making the final decision alone.

Allow independent evaluation.

Document the evaluation criteria.

Compare vendors transparently.

Make the final decision based on objective technical and organizational requirements.

# MASTER ANSWER–26
Ethical Leadership in IT Organizations
Applicable to: Q13(a), Q13(b), Q13(c), Q13(d)

## English Answer
Ethical leadership in an IT organization means creating a work environment where honesty, safety,
privacy, security, fairness, and public interest are taken seriously.

An ethical leader should not measure success only by how quickly a product is released. The leader
should also consider the effects of decisions on users, employees, customers, and society.

# 1. Clear Policies

The organization should have clear policies for security, privacy, safety, responsible data use,
compliance, and reporting concerns.

# 2. Fair Incentives

Reward systems should not encourage employees to ignore security or privacy problems just to meet
deadlines. Quality, security, privacy, reliability, and responsible risk management should also be valued.

# 3. Training and Awareness

Employees should receive regular training on professional ethics, security, privacy, compliance, and
reporting procedures.

# 4. Open Communication

Employees should be able to raise technical and ethical concerns without fear of unfair punishment.

# 5. Transparency

Leaders should communicate important risks, limitations, and decisions honestly. Problems should not be
hidden simply to protect the organization's image.

6. Accountability

Responsibilities should be clearly assigned. When an ethical or technical failure occurs, the organization
should investigate the cause and take corrective action.


7. Protection from Retaliation

Employees who raise genuine security, privacy, safety, or compliance concerns should have a safe
reporting mechanism and should not be treated as obstacles to progress.

Performance targets can unintentionally encourage unethical behavior. If employees are rewarded only
for fast release, they may hide vulnerabilities or avoid reporting problems. Therefore, ethical leadership
must align incentives with responsible professional behavior.

A practical framework is:

Clear policy → Ethical targets → Training → Safe reporting → Management review → Independent
escalation → Corrective action → Accountability
